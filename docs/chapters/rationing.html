Chapter 4

Who gets the last unit?

Compare three ways to allocate two scarce units among four households.

Your decision. A shop has two generators for a one-day outage. Four households value a day of power at 120, 90, 60, and 30. Each unit costs 20 to supply. Should the shop raise the price, hold a lottery, or serve the queue?

Compare who gets power and what allocation costs

At a price of 75, only the first two households buy. Their combined value is 210. Subtract 40 of supply cost to obtain total surplus of 170. The shop earns 110 and consumers keep 60. The price splits the surplus between buyers and seller. Charging it consumes no generator capacity.

At a price of 25 all four apply. An equal lottery without replacement gives each a one-half chance. Expected gross value is half of 300, or 150. Expected total surplus is 110 and the shop earns 10. Each household gets a chance, including the two who would decline at 75.

Count waiting time as a real cost

Suppose the queue delivers the units to the 120 and 60 households, and all four spend time worth 10 each waiting. What changes?

Work through the decision

Gross value is 180. Subtract supply cost of 40 and waiting cost of 40 to obtain surplus of 100. A one-unit purchase limit would prevent a household from taking both, but all households already want only one. To prioritize medical need, identify eligible households and explicitly measure reach. Willingness to pay also reflects purchasing power.

What would you ask the shop manager to decide before allocating the generators?

Compare your recommendation

If the aim is highest total surplus under the stated valuations, the HK$75 price directs the units to the two highest-value users. If emergency need takes priority, establish how staff can identify eligible households before choosing an allocation rule.
