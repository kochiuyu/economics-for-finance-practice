Chapter 28 · Hong Kong and exchange-rate regimes

What does the currency link protect?

Compare the exchange cost of a US-dollar invoice with the Hong Kong dollar interest bill needed to finance it.

Your decision. A Hong Kong importer owes US$1 million in 90 days and collects revenue in Hong Kong dollars. Its manager says, “With the currency link, we can leave the interest expense out of our stress test.” What would you put in the cash forecast?

Translate the invoice before adding finance costs

Quote the exchange rate in HK dollars per US dollar. A higher number makes the US-dollar invoice more expensive. From a reference of 7.80, an exchange rate of 7.85 adds HK$50,000 to the invoice. The importer also carries a separate HK$8 million working-capital loan over these 90 days. Assume simple interest and a 360-day year. The table shows invoice payment plus interest. The working-capital principal is common to every scenario and falls due later.

Scenario HK$ per US$ Annual loan rate Invoice + 90-day interest
Reference 7.80 4% HK$7,880,000
Weak-side exchange rate and tighter funding 7.85 6% HK$7,970,000
Hypothetical floating regime 8.20 4% HK$8,280,000

In the second row, HK$50,000 comes from exchange translation and HK$40,000 from the extra interest (8 million × 0.02 × 90/360). A treasury forecast reporting only the invoice misses almost half the HK$90,000 increase. Rates and loan terms are illustrative. The floating scenario is a counterfactual comparison.

How does pressure on the link reach banks?

Under the Linked Exchange Rate System, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority undertakes to buy US dollars from banks at HK$7.75 and sell US dollars to banks at HK$7.85. At the weak side, a bank delivers HK dollars to the HKMA in exchange for US dollars. The banking system’s Aggregate Balance contracts. Scarcer Hong Kong dollar liquidity puts upward pressure on local interest rates. At the strong side, the transaction goes in the opposite direction and the Aggregate Balance expands. These undertakings face banks. A customer’s retail conversion includes the bank’s pricing. HKMA explanation of the adjustment mechanism

The open-economy model in the lecture links three policy choices. With mobile capital and a commitment to the exchange-rate link, the scope to set an independent domestic interest-rate path is limited. Local credit spreads, liquidity, and loan reset terms still matter for how much and how quickly a particular borrower pays.

Would fixing the exchange rate on the invoice settle the manager’s question?

Assume a forward fixes the invoice at 7.80 with settlement on the payment date and no extra fee. If the loan resets to 6%, interest is HK$120,000 and the combined cash requirement is HK$7,920,000. The forward fixes one component. Ask separately about the loan’s reset date, the spread charged on renewal, and whether tighter funding may also reduce customers’ orders.

Finish the essentials route

Write a three-sentence treasury note. State the currency and payment date of the invoice. Quantify the exchange and interest components under one stress scenario. Name one assumption that would change the result. A manager can then decide whether to hedge the invoice, fix the loan rate, or address both exposures.

Convert an FX move and a rate move into comparable cash amounts

On the US$1 million invoice, HK$0.01 per dollar changes cash needs by HK$10,000. On the HK$8 million loan over 90 days on the stated basis, a 0.5-point annual rate move also changes interest by HK$10,000. These sensitivities apply to separate exposures. Confirm each settlement or reset date before combining shocks.
