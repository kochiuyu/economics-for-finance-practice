Chapter 10 · Behavioral finance

Which holding would you sell?

Make a sale decision twice, then test whether a change in the purchase history justifies changing your order.

Your decision. You must raise HK$100 today. You own one share each of A and B, both trading at HK$100. A cost you HK$70 and B cost HK$130. Which share would you sell?

Make the trade twice

Assume no taxes, fees, or restrictions. From today onward, A and B have identical payoff distributions and identical exposures to the rest of your portfolio. You cannot affect their prices. Choose before opening the second account statement.

Open the second account statement

Everything about today’s holdings and future prospects is the same. This time you paid HK$130 for A and HK$70 for B. Would you switch which share you sell? Write down which fact changed your decision.

Selling either share raises HK$100. In the first account, selling A records a HK$30 gain and leaves a HK$30 unrealized loss on B. Selling B reverses the accounting treatment. In both cases you finish with HK$100 cash and one share with the same future risk. If you chose to realize the gain in both versions, your sale followed the purchase price. That pattern is called the disposition effect. One hypothetical choice is insufficient to diagnose anyone’s preferences.

When should purchase history matter?

Suppose losses can now offset taxable gains immediately and the tax rate on either is 20%. In the first account, realizing A’s gain creates a HK$6 liability. Realizing B’s loss saves HK$6 if you have eligible gains to offset. The tax cash flows differ by HK$12 today. If the remaining position will also be sold, its tax basis still matters for future liabilities. The example gives a reason to ask about taxes before explaining an actual investor’s sale as a bias.

Why might a price gap survive?

Two securities promise the same cash at a known settlement date. One trades at 100 and the other at 105. An arbitrageur buys the cheaper claim and shorts the dearer one. Upon convergence the gross gain is 5 per pair. Now suppose the prices move to 95 and 110 before settlement. How much additional loss must the strategy withstand while waiting?

Trace the cash and collateral

The long position loses 5 and buying back the short costs 5 more. The interim mark-to-market loss is 10. If the broker demands that loss in cash and the desk has only 6 of spare collateral, the desk faces a funding gap of 4. Forced closure at these prices locks in the loss. Even if the trade survives, borrowing fees reduce its final gain.

The two decisions answer different questions. A preference for realizing gains can explain an individual trade. Explaining a persistent market price also requires evidence that enough investors trade in that direction and that other traders face costs or constraints when taking the opposite side.

What belongs on the sale ticket when either share raises the cash?

Compare your recommendation

With identical prospects and no tax effects, either sale raises HK$100. If losses can offset gains immediately, selling B in the original account improves today’s tax cash flow by HK$12 relative to selling A. Verify the offset before relying on it.
