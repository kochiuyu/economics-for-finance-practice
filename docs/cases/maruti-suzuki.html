Case seminar · Maruti Suzuki

Should the automaker commit before policy is known?

Compare the value of deterring entry with the value of waiting for evidence about electric-car demand.

The original CUHK course assigned Veena Keshav Pailwar’s Should Maruti Suzuki Invest in Electric Cars? The classroom question was whether the automaker should enter now or wait, given rivals’ decisions and policy uncertainty. The exercise below uses a fictional manufacturer and independently constructed payoffs. Every figure needed for the decision appears here. Published case, W18345

Your decision. Approve an irreversible electric-car launch today or wait for a policy announcement? Waiting reveals demand but gives a rival the first opportunity to launch. Give the board a recommendation and the probability at which you would reverse it.

What the board knows today

Call the two states strong demand and weak demand. The probability of strong demand is 60%. All figures are incremental net present values in the same illustrative units. A firm that stays out earns zero. If a firm commits early it must produce in either state. After the announcement, any uncommitted firm observes the state and earlier launch decisions. Both firms have the same state-contingent payoffs.

Outcome for a launching firm Strong demand Weak demand
Sole producer 12 −6
Both produce 4 −9

If you launch today

After strong demand is revealed, the rival can earn 4 by joining or zero by staying out. It joins. After weak demand, joining would lose 9, so it stays out. Your payoff is therefore 4 in the strong state and −6 in the weak state. Early entry cannot deter a rival when the market is large enough to support two firms.

If you wait

The rival chooses first after the announcement, and you can respond. In the strong state it anticipates that you will enter after it launches. Its resulting payoff of 4 exceeds zero, so it launches and you follow. In the weak state even a sole producer loses 6. Both stay out. Write down the expected payoff of waiting before comparing it with early entry.

Resolve the board decision

At a 60% chance of strong demand, launching now yields 0.6 × 4 + 0.4 × (−6) = 0. Waiting yields 0.6 × 4 + 0.4 × 0 = 2.4. For any strong-demand probability p below one, waiting gains 6(1 − p). Both choices yield 4 at p = 1. Under these terms there is no interior probability at which early entry becomes better.

Negotiate an exclusive contract

An alternative early launch includes exclusive distribution. The rival cannot sell in either state. The contract costs 2 up front in present-value units. If you decline, the original waiting option remains available. Does the contract change your vote at 60%? What about at 50%?

Find the probability that changes the vote

With exclusivity the expected payoff is 12p − 6(1 − p) − 2 = 18p − 8. Waiting yields 4p. Exclusivity wins when 14p exceeds 8, or p exceeds 4/7, about 57.1%. At 60%, exclusive entry yields 2.8 against 2.4 from waiting. At 50%, it yields 1 against 2. The board’s advantage at 60% is only 0.4, so validating the demand probability and the scope of the exclusive rights matters.

Board memo. State who moves when, what the rival can observe, and whether it can profit after your launch. Recommend waiting under the ordinary terms. At the stated 60% probability, approve exclusive entry only on the stated enforceable terms and cost.
